Coursework

Scientific Communication

Taught by program chair Dr. Nicole Campbell and Dr. Adrian Owen.

About the course

Communicating Science in the 21st Century built a practical toolkit for reaching the very different audiences a researcher has to write for — peer reviewers, funding panels, colleagues in other fields, and the public. Almost every session ended in something written, pitched, or posted, so the ideas below were never left as theory for long.

What the course covered

Knowing your audience

Every other choice follows from who is listening. We mapped the full range of audiences a scientist writes for, then practised the hardest case — a reader with no background at all — by writing a 250-word layman summary of a research article for a general newspaper readership.

Clear and concise writing

Writing was treated as a sequence rather than a single act: free writing, organizing, drafting, reviewing, revising, editing. Rewriting overloaded sentences together at the whiteboard made the lesson concrete — most scientific prose gets clearer by deletion.

Proposals, pitches and panels

A strong proposal answers three questions: what you want to do, why it matters, and how you will do it. Alongside the Canadian Tri-Council agencies and their requirements, we heard directly from Dr. Owen on how grant panels actually arrive at a decision.

Publication and peer review

We followed a manuscript from journal selection through to decision, comparing single-blind, double-blind, open, published, post-publication and collaborative review. Writing a review of a peer's work, then a response letter answering a review of my own, taught the etiquette of critique far better than reading about it would have.

Open access, preprints and predatory journals

Beall's List, the long argument over MDPI, and the role preprints played during COVID-19 all showed how much of publishing runs on judgment rather than rules. The open-access scenarios we worked through — a $3,000 fee at a top journal against free publication somewhere quieter — had no clean answers, which was the point.

Generative AI in scientific writing

We stress-tested AI-written scientific prose and kept finding the same faults: generic, overconfident, no limitations, no evidence. The course closed by asking each of us to set our own guidelines for these tools — where they genuinely help with clarity and structure, and where using one would mean signing my name to something I could not vouch for.
