Scientific Communication

Taught by program chair Dr. Nicole Campbell and Dr. Adrian Owen.

About the course

Communicating Science in the 21st Century built a practical toolkit for the very different audiences a researcher has to write for — peer reviewers, funding panels, colleagues in other fields, and the public. Six threads ran through the term, working outward from the reader: how to be understood, how to persuade, how research becomes public, and what that costs.

What I took away

The course covered the full spread of ways science actually reaches people — pitch presentations, podcasts, debates, social media posts — and made the case that the choice of form matters as much as what goes into it. That choice only works if you know who is on the other end: what they are interested in, and how much they already know. Getting that judgment right is what decides whether a message lands or stalls.

Working through the structure of scientific publishing, and writing peer reviews myself, showed me how many of the mistakes writers make are avoidable ones — imprecision, and guidelines followed loosely. The standard I took from it is easy to state and hard to meet: a piece of scientific writing should be clear enough to be understood on the first read.

Where my thinking changed

Before this course, my instinct with any short presentation was to simplify as far as I could. Every workshop I had been through rewarded making things easy to follow, so I treated simplicity as the goal in itself rather than a means to one.

The session on pitches and proposals took that apart. The target is not the simplest possible version of an idea — it is the version calibrated to a particular audience's interests and background knowledge. Oversimplifying does not make a topic accessible; it just fails in the opposite direction.

A topic made too simple is as poorly communicated as one framed too complexly.

That has changed how I approach presenting a scientific topic to anyone, whether the audience is a full room or a single person.
